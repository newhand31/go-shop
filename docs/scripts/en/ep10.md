# Episode 10 | Singles' Day, 8:00 p.m. (voice-over draft v0, English channel)

- Target length: about 10 minutes (about 1,350 spoken words plus screen-only beats and code on screen)
- The one main concept: **a channel with a fixed capacity is back-pressure.** A Redis counter turns away the people who can no longer win, before they reach the database; a bounded channel plus `select … default` turns away the people the shop has no room for, on the spot, instead of letting them pile up out of sight. The comparison that matters: `BlockingQueue.offer` / `TryWrite` versus the waiting versions (`put` / `WriteAsync`) is the difference between queueing and back-pressure

## 0:50 Time anchor

> It's 8:31 p.m., thirty-one minutes into the sale, and our connection pool is full, one monitor is left, and nineteen thousand requests are saying "please wait".
> Three things today: take a stock counter in Redis, so people who can't win never reach the database; line up everyone who can in a channel with a fixed length, and answer "busy" on the spot when it's full; and cap the connection pool, with fewer workers than connections. Then we prove three things: the pre-deduction never oversells, a full channel rejects, and database connections have a ceiling.
> And we learn one thing: **a channel with a capacity is back-pressure.**

## 1:40 How we'd do it in Spring (and why Go won't play along)

【畫面】Split screen: left, a Spring `@Transactional` `buy()` behind a `ThreadPoolExecutor` whose queue is a `LinkedBlockingQueue` with no capacity; below it, C#: `Channel.CreateUnbounded<Order>()`. Right, an empty `flashsale.go`.

> Coming from Java: hand it to an `ExecutorService` and let it queue. C# folks: `Channel.CreateUnbounded`, one side writes, one side reads.
> Both work. And both ship with a default that turns this night into an incident: **the queue has no limit.** A `LinkedBlockingQueue` without a capacity defaults to `Integer.MAX_VALUE`. `CreateUnbounded` tells you in the name.
> An unbounded queue doesn't solve the problem. It moves the problem. The connection pool stops blowing up, and memory starts climbing instead: every waiting request holds a thread or a goroutine, a socket, and a person staring at a spinner. In three minutes the wait goes from two seconds to three minutes. They won't say "what a patient system". They'll hit refresh.
> **And then twenty thousand people become forty thousand.** Our queue is very polite: it rejects nobody. It's the most courteous outage you'll ever see.

> What we need isn't a faster queue. It's a queue that can be full, and that says no when it is.

## 3:30 Part one: pre-deduct in Redis, so sold-out people never reach the database

【畫面】`internal/repository/flashsale.go`, the `reserveScript` (from line 20).

```lua
local left = redis.call('GET', KEYS[1])
if not left then return -1 end
if tonumber(left) < tonumber(ARGV[1]) then return 0 end
redis.call('DECRBY', KEYS[1], ARGV[1])
return 1
```

> First idea: ten monitors, twenty thousand people, so 19,990 of them cannot win. Why make each of them lock a row in the database just to hear "gone"?
> So we put a counter in front of it: a number in Redis, ten. Everyone takes one on the way in. Got one? You may continue. Didn't? "Sold out", and the database never learns you exist.
> The whole question is how to take. `GET`, check, then `DECRBY`? Two commands, a gap between them, two people see "one left", both subtract, and the number goes negative. That's episode 6's oversell again, one network hop wider.
> So we use a Lua script. Redis runs a script as a single step; nobody's command can sneak in between. Check and subtract are one action. Three answers: minus one, no sale open; zero, not enough; one, you got it.
> Note what the counter is: a **gate**, not the truth. The truth is still the database: whether an order exists is decided by episode 6's `FOR UPDATE` transaction. The counter has one job, to keep 19,990 people who cannot win out of the building.

> If the database step fails later, the unit goes back: that's the compensation, `Release`, one `INCRBY`.
> This is the first time in this series that we fixed a Go project with code that isn't Go. Sounds odd, Lua living in a Go project. Except it doesn't live in ours. It lives in Redis; we just asked it to do one thing in one step. **We didn't learn Lua. We borrowed five lines of it.**

## 5:45 Part two: a bounded channel and `select … default`

【畫面】`internal/service/flashsale.go`, `Buy`, the `select` (from line 106).

```go
select {
case f.queue <- job:
default:
    f.release(job)
    return Order{}, ErrBusy
}
```

> Everyone holding a unit now goes to the database. Not all at once, that would flatten the pool, so there's a channel in the middle: `make(chan *flashJob, 64)`. Capacity sixty-four.
> The usual write is `f.queue <- job`. When the channel is full, that line **waits**. The goroutine sits there, the connection hangs, the browser spins. That's `CreateUnbounded` again, just with the queue hidden somewhere you can't see.
> So we use `select` with a `default`. Meaning: if it fits, put it in. If it doesn't fit, don't wait: take the `default` branch. There we give back the unit we just reserved and return `ErrBusy`. The handler turns that into a 503 with `Retry-After: 1`.
> Java folks, that's `queue.offer(job)`, not `queue.put(job)`. C# folks, it's `writer.TryWrite(job)`, and you decide what `false` means. One word of difference, and it's the difference between queueing and back-pressure.
> This is the most important line in the episode. **Back-pressure isn't queueing more efficiently. It's saying no when you're full.**

> Sure, being told no doesn't feel great. But think about it: the person at the door hears "try again in a second" right now. The person in the queue hears "failed" in three minutes. Which one sounds like customer service?
> Our checkout finally learned the hardest sentence in support: "It's busy right now, please come back in a moment." It just says it faster than any support agent.

【畫面】One more detail in the code: the customer who closes the tab while queued.

> One more case: what if a queued buyer leaves? Their reservation can't sit there forever. Each queued job carries a flag, flipped with a `CompareAndSwap`: if the worker claims it first, it's the worker's; if the buyer gives up first, it's the buyer's. Whoever wins is the only one who gives the unit back, exactly once. The worker later skips the abandoned job and saves a database call.

## 8:20 Part three: only a few workers touch the database

【畫面】`FlashSale.Run`; `postgres.OpenWithMaxConns`; `cmd/api/main.go` with `DB_MAX_CONNS`.

> The last piece: who takes work off the channel? Four worker goroutines, one loop each: read from the channel, place the order, answer. At most four are in the database at the same moment.
> The pool gets a ceiling too: `OpenWithMaxConns`, default ten. PostgreSQL's default `max_connections` is a hundred, and every API instance may spend up to ten of them. **That ceiling is a promise we make to the database.** If you want more connections, you wait; you don't open more.
> The worker count is deliberately smaller than the pool. The pool also serves episode 8's mail worker, episode 9's janitor, and everyone who isn't in the sale. Add the numbers up and you have the shop's total capacity: four in the database, sixty-four waiting, and the sixty-ninth person is asked, very nicely, to come back later.

> The shop finally has a capacity. **For the first time it isn't decided by "however far we get before it falls over".** Until tonight our capacity was whatever number was on the screen when it crashed.

> Starting them follows the same rule as episodes 8 and 9: whoever starts a goroutine stops it. The `workerCtx` in `main.go` now covers three goroutines, and the same `defer` cancels, waits for all three, and only then lets `pool.Close` run.

## 10:00 Proof: three things

【畫面】`go test ./internal/service -run Flash -v`, then `go test -tags=integration ./internal/service -run Integration -v`, all green.

> One: the pre-deduction never oversells. Five monitors, a hundred people at the same instant. Result: exactly five orders, ninety-five "sold out", database stock at zero and not below, and the database saw five order attempts, not a hundred. The counter stopped the other ninety-five.
> Two: a full channel rejects. In a unit test: one worker, one queue slot. The first buyer is in the database, the second waits, the third arrives and is answered `ErrBusy` **immediately**. The database call count stays at one, and the third buyer's reserved unit went back. The integration test then runs against a real database: two workers, five queue slots, two hundred buyers at once. Some are turned away; sold plus turned away is exactly two hundred; and the Redis counter equals the stock minus the orders. Nobody who was turned away cost us a unit.
> Three: database connections have a ceiling. Pool capped at four, three workers, a hundred and fifty buyers at once. We sample the pool every millisecond. Busy connections peaked at three at most, and the pool never grew past four.

> By the way, we measured the pool's `AcquiredConns`, not the number of requests. Requests make a nicer graph. The pool is what the database actually feels.

## 11:45 Three takeaways

> Let's wrap up.
> **One**, keep the people who cannot win out of the database: a Redis Lua pre-deduction, check and subtract in a single step. The counter is a gate, not the truth.
> **Two**, back-pressure means saying no when you're full: a bounded channel and `select … default`. Not a bigger queue, not faster workers.
> **Three**, write the capacity down: pool ceiling, worker count, queue length. Add them up, and that's how many people the shop can hold.
>
> The boss's next message: "Last night some people said checkout was slow. Which step was it?"
> Ship it small, ship it tested. See you at the next incident.

## Appendix B: open questions

- Laugh timestamps are estimates; re-time them against the real voice-over length before recording.
- The Redis counter and the database stock drift apart: episode 9's janitor returns expired orders' stock to the database but not to the counter. During a sale the counter can only be lower than the truth, never higher: it under-sells rather than oversells. Not handled here, no test.
- If the process dies after the reservation and before the order, that unit is never returned (no TTL, no reconciliation). Same direction: under-sell, never oversell.
- The sale is opened with `SET NX`, so a restart does not refill the counter. If Redis is wiped, the flash-sale route answers 500 (`ErrSaleNotOpen`) and does not reopen by itself.
- There are no accounts, so "one per customer" cannot be enforced: one person can buy all ten. Not handled here.
- The flash-sale route is not in the OpenAPI contract (like the payment callback, a separate chi route). It sits behind episode 4's per-IP rate limiter, so a single-IP load test has to raise `RATE_LIMIT_RPS` and `RATE_LIMIT_BURST`.
- `Retry-After` is a fixed 1 second, not derived from the queue length.
- A queued buyer has no timeout of their own, only their connection's `ctx`. `ErrBusy` also comes back when `Run` has stopped; the full shutdown story is episode 12.
- Defaults: workers 4, queue 64, `DB_MAX_CONNS` 10; tune with `FLASH_SALE_PRODUCT_ID`, `FLASH_SALE_STOCK`, `FLASH_SALE_WORKERS`, `FLASH_SALE_QUEUE`, `DB_MAX_CONNS`. Without `FLASH_SALE_PRODUCT_ID` the route does not exist.
- The sign-off is the fixed line from §6.1.
- The next-episode teaser only states the symptom (which step was slow), not the fix.
